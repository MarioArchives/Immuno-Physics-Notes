Section X Discussion

Quantitative immunology is wider than the topics covered here. Some active areas are left out, each for a specific reason, and the links between the topics that are covered should become much clearer in the coming years.

What is left out

Broad as the preceding chapters are, they do not cover everything that counts as quantitative immunology. Transcriptomics, the measurement of which genes each cell is expressing, is one example. Its methods are quantitative, but experiments applying it to the immune system are only beginning to produce quantitative models that connect to a physical understanding. The area moves fast enough that this assessment may already be out of date.

Some widely used approaches appear without an account of how they work. Machine learning is one, and the theory of stochastic gene expression and biochemical regulation is another. Dedicated reviews written for physicists already cover both and supply that background. Major experimental advances, imaging among them, get only brief mentions because they are just now being used to test quantitative models.

The picture to take away is of a lively field moving in many directions at once. Any ordering of the material involves hard choices, since nearly every topic touches several others. Those links can be expected to be explored much more fully in the coming years, as careful experimental measurement and the validation of theoretical models become standard practice.

Where to go next

The chapters are designed to be read in any order. For the physical limits on a single binding event, start with the physical chemistry of binding. For the sharpest puzzle in the field, how a T cell turns a few-fold difference in binding time into a difference of \(10^{5}\) or more in its response, read about antigen discrimination. Signaling through space is carried by cytokines, while decisions and bistability are questions of cell fate. For statistics of diversity and clone sizes, read about repertoires and lymphocyte population dynamics. For evolution, follow affinity maturation and then the co-evolution of pathogens and hosts. The map of scales shows where each of these sits.

In short

The coverage is deliberately incomplete. It skips transcriptomics, which is still turning quantitative data into physical models, and methods such as machine learning and stochastic gene expression that other reviews already cover for physicists. It touches only briefly on new experimental techniques such as imaging, which are now being used to test the models. The many links between topics should become clearer as rigorous measurement and model validation become routine.
